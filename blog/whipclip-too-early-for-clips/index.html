Clips Rule the Content Universe. Whipclip Was Just Too Early.

By Michael Donnon · August 20, 2026 · 6 min read

At Whipclip we built one of the only legal destinations for IP-licensed clips from major networks and sports leagues. A decade later, clips run the content universe — just not in the shape we bet on. So I keep asking myself: was this a Quibi moment for the clip space?

Let me start with what we got right, because it's the whole reason the question is worth asking. People don't consume shows anymore. They consume moments — the goal, the punchline, the plot twist, the ten seconds worth texting to a friend. Whipclip was built on that insight before it was obvious, and we did the genuinely hard part: we cleared the rights. Licensed clips from real networks and real sports leagues, legally, at scale. Almost nobody else had built that.

We were right about the format

The thesis has aged extremely well. Short-form clips didn't just find an audience — they became the dominant way an entire generation discovers, watches, and shares video. If you'd shown our early decks to someone today, the read on consumer behavior would look prescient.

And the rights work we did was ahead of its time in a way I still don't think the market fully appreciates. Clearing premium clips legally is a brutal problem — the licensing, the attribution, the revenue splits. We solved a version of it years before "who owns this clip" became an industry-wide headache.

But we built a destination when the world wanted a behavior

Here's the miss. The format won; our venue for it didn't. Clips took over as a native behavior inside platforms people already lived in — TikTok, Reels, Shorts — powered by creators, not by a standalone premium app you had to be convinced to open. We asked people to come to the clips. The winners brought the clips to where people already were.

That's not a small distinction. It's the difference between building a product and building a destination — and destinations are expensive, capital-hungry things to stand up, especially when every clip carries a license fee.

So — a Quibi moment for clips?

I think there's something to it. Quibi wasn't wrong that short-form premium video had a future; it was wrong about how and where people wanted to consume it, and it spent enormous money betting on the wrong container before the behavior was ready. Whipclip's version was gentler and far less expensive, but the pattern rhymes: right format, right instinct about clips, wrong shape — a big, rights-heavy destination, arguably too big too early. The behavior needed a few more years and a different container than the one we could build with the capital and timing we had.

Being early looks identical to being wrong — right up until the moment it doesn't.

The pivot was smart. But it shelved the consumer thesis.

Whipclip became Whip Media, and that was a genuinely good call. It took the hardest-won asset we had — deep expertise in content rights and licensing data — and turned it into a durable B2B business serving the studios and networks themselves. As a company decision, the pivot worked.

But it did mean the original consumer bet got shelved rather than solved. The question "what if licensed clips were the destination" never really got answered. We changed the question instead.

Would doubling down on clips have been bigger?

Honest answer: maybe. The consumer clip opportunity turned out to be enormous — arguably one of the biggest shifts in media of the last decade. But capturing it required two things we didn't have at the time: the patience to survive until the behavior matured, and the right model — creator-forward, distributed instead of destination, rights-cleared but not rights-heavy. That's a materially different company than the one we had the money and the moment to build.

So I don't beat myself up over it. "Too early" is only a failure if you stop paying attention. The thesis was sound. The timing and the shape were the problem — and those are solvable the second time around.

Which is why I'm still betting on clips

The unfinished problem — premium, rights-cleared clips that creators and audiences can actually use, with the people who own the IP getting paid fairly — is exactly what I'm building toward now with VETA and Clipa.tv. Same core belief that made Whipclip worth doing in the first place, with a decade of hindsight about the shape it should take: rights-forward, creator-and-audience-native, built for how people actually consume clips today rather than how we hoped they would in 2015.

Whipclip wasn't a failure of vision. It was a lesson in timing. And the best thing you can do with a lesson like that is go use it.
